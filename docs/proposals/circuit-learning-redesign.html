01 / The current game

A good puzzle foundation.
An incomplete teaching sequence.

My assessment: it is not yet well scaffolded for a beginner learning circuits. The six chambers gradually expand the available parts, and physical construction makes outcomes visible. But players can often follow a hint or find the right brightness without understanding why their circuit works.

Yes, the battery, bulb, wire, switch, and resistor already exist in the notebook. They currently appear when the player visits a room that contains or offers them. That records access, not a meaningful discovery. A circuit itself has no introductory concept entry.

02 / Recommended progression

Twelve levels. Six places.

Give each existing room two short, distinct activities. The second becomes available after the first, using a second work surface or a visibly reconfigured panel. Open the room’s exit after both. This creates room to learn without requiring twelve new environments.

The pattern is encounter → experiment → use independently. Early tasks have fewer parts and visible contacts. Later tasks remove the recipe, change the arrangement, and ask for evidence. Equations become concise descriptions of things the player has already observed.

Room 01 · Wake

Story: intake, first light, first small victory
01First lightDiscover the objects and make a complete circuit.

Experience

A dark intake desk has a battery, a lamp, and one connected lead. Tandem’s pointer introduces each part as the player handles it. Inspecting the lamp reveals its two electrically separate terminals and the filament connecting them. The task is simply: “Light the desk.”

The player adds the return wire, then lifts and reconnects either lead. They see that both battery terminals and both lamp contacts belong to the path.

Discoveries

Battery: supplies energy to the circuit. Wire: its metal core connects parts. Bulb: converts electrical energy into light and heat. Circuit: an arrangement of connected electrical components; a closed loop provides a complete path through them.

Do not begin with numerical voltage, resistance, or current. The battery does not manufacture charge, and a single connection is not enough.

Solution & evidence

Connect the unused battery terminal to the unused lamp terminal. A 6 V source across the 12 Ω training lamp gives the expected light. Require closing, opening, and restoring the path once; the notebook records the visible changes. A working loop is the first proof, not yet a claim of mastery.

Small transfer: move the break to the other side of the bulb. The same repair works because continuity around the whole loop matters.

Tandem: “Your parents asked us to start with the fundamentals. I argued for a lower starting point. Unfortunately, we could not find one beneath ‘lamp.’ Let’s establish what each object contributes.”

02Break the pathDiscover switches, exposed contacts, and open circuits.

Experience

The desk is lit, but the next panel needs a controllable light. Introduce a switch with a visible moving metal bridge. The player puts it into the existing loop and opens and closes it.

A spare connector can touch a metal contact or rest against the wire’s insulation. Only the metal connection closes the path. Make the snap target and failed contact visually unambiguous.

Learning target

A switch changes the continuity of the conducting path; it does not produce electricity. In this simple steady DC loop, an opening anywhere stops current everywhere.

Notebook: unlock Switch after operating it in a live loop. Add an open/closed pair of pictures to Circuit. Update Wire: “The metal core conducts; the coating insulates. Connect at exposed contacts.”

Solution & evidence

Replace one wire section with a switch, connecting a lead to each terminal. Close it to light the bulb, open it to extinguish the bulb, and restore it. A lead touching insulation must be moved onto exposed metal.

Small transfer: the switch moves to the other side of the bulb. Predict “off” with a single optional response, then operate it and observe. Gate completion on the actual demonstration, with immediate retries and no penalty for a wrong prediction.

Tandem: “A switch is a gap with excellent branding. Put it in the path. Then find out whether the circuit cares which side of the lamp the gap is on.”

Room 02 · Contact

Story: earn responsibility for a small station system
03Build it yourselfReconstruct a circuit and recognize its schematic.

Experience

An empty maintenance bench needs a working service light. Offer only known parts: battery, bulb, wire, and switch. The goal describes the behavior: “A light you can turn off.” No ghost circuit shows the answer.

Once built, its physical parts briefly align with their schematic symbols. A second panel shows the same simple circuit as a diagram, with parts arranged differently on the bench.

Learning target

Connections determine circuit behavior; a drawing’s shape and component positions do not. Wire crossings are not automatically junctions. Use junction dots consistently.

Notebook: add symbols alongside the discovered objects and save the player’s first schematic. The circuit entry shows one physical arrangement and its equivalent diagram.

Solution & evidence

Build one loop containing the battery, bulb, and switch. Check that opening the switch turns the lamp off. Then reproduce a rearranged diagram with no highlighted contacts. Many shapes and component orders are valid.

Independent check: the second panel starts empty and rotated. Its completion matters more than the first, assisted build. If help is requested, provide it and later offer another equivalent layout.

Tandem: “The diagram has been rotated. A devastating development for anyone whose scientific method was ‘remember where the wire went.’ Fortunately, we have you.”

04Nothing disappearsMeasure current and separate charge from energy.

Experience

Tandem asks whether the lamp uses up the current. Introduce a removable inline ammeter. First, a clearly marked test gap teaches that the meter must join the path. Move it to a second gap on the other side of the lamp and compare.

A restrained optional overlay depicts charge already present throughout the conductors. Motion starts around the loop together on the game’s time scale; there is no solitary packet racing out of the battery to wake the lamp.

Learning target

Electric charge is a property of particles such as electrons; it is not the same thing as energy. Current is the rate of charge passing a point: I = ΔQ/Δt. In a single steady loop, the rate is the same before and after the lamp. The lamp transfers energy, not a supply of disappearing charge.

Notebook: unlock Ammeter and a Current note with “ampere = coulomb per second.” Explain that animated arrows represent conventional current; electrons in metal drift the opposite way.

Solution & evidence

At 6 V with a 12 Ω lamp, both positions read approximately 0.50 A. Open the switch: both become zero. Require two correctly placed measurements and that open-switch observation. Do not require Ohm’s law yet; these are observed values.

Independent check: move the meter to an unmarked third section of the same loop. Its reading remains 0.50 A. Briefly connect the equal readings to charge conservation.

Tandem: “The lamp has produced light without eating the current. You can confirm that with the meter. I recommend evidence; it is much harder for me to argue with.”

Room 03 · Assembly

Story: qualify to adjust the station’s training supply
05What the battery changesVoltage is an energy difference per unit charge.

Experience

Add a voltmeter with two probes. The player measures across the source, across the bulb, and across one uninterrupted wire. Use paired highlights so “across two points” is unmistakable.

Open the series switch and measure the battery again. The source still reads 6 V while the ammeter reads zero. Then measure across the open switch. Introduce a selectable 3 V / 6 V training supply only after the probe interaction is clear.

Learning target

Potential difference measures a change of electrical potential energy per charge: ΔV = ΔU/q. A 6 V battery provides 6 J of energy per coulomb passing through it in the ideal model. Voltage can exist without current.

Notebook: unlock Voltmeter; upgrade Battery with its voltage meaning. The meter belongs across a component, unlike an ammeter in its path. Ideal wires have negligible voltage drop.

Solution & evidence

With the loop closed: source 6 V, lamp approximately 6 V, wire approximately 0 V. With the switch open: source 6 V, switch approximately 6 V, current 0 A. At 3 V with the loop closed, this lamp is dimmer but still carries current. Record the distinction between “energized” and “at its design rating.”

Independent check: reverse the voltmeter probes across the battery. The sign reverses; the battery and brightness do not change. Select two points to answer “Is the battery dead, or is the loop open?”

Tandem: “Six volts and no current. The battery is not confused. You are measuring two different things. One tells us the energy difference per charge; the other tells us how much charge passes each second.”

06Resistance has a patternDiscover a resistor; measure before using V = IR.

Experience

A calibration rack introduces a fixed resistor in place of the lamp. With R = 12 Ω, vary the supply through 3, 6, and 9 V. The notebook captures readings; the player chooses voltage for the horizontal axis and current for the vertical axis, then places the three points on a small instrument screen.

Keep 6 V fixed and exchange the resistor for 24 Ω. Only one independent variable changes at a time.

Learning target

For an ohmic resistor at fixed conditions, I is proportional to V. At fixed voltage, increasing R reduces I. Name V = IR after that pattern appears, with units and a worked reading from the player’s own data.

Notebook: discover Resistor, then add Ohm’s law and the I–V graph. Its slope is 1/R. Distinguish ideal fixed-resistance training components from real filament lamps whose resistance changes with temperature.

Solution & evidence

For 12 Ω, the readings are 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 A at 3, 6, and 9 V. At 6 V, changing to 24 Ω gives 0.25 A. To obtain 0.25 A from a 9 V source in a fresh single-resistor circuit, select 36 Ω.

Independent check: choose and justify the new resistor using the measured pattern, then test it. A correct selection alone is recorded as a solved task; a changed-value check provides stronger evidence of application.

Tandem: “You have produced a straight line. Usually a modest artistic achievement. Here it tells us something useful: double the voltage, and this resistor carries twice the current.”

Room 04 · Balance

Story: restore a corridor and protect its equipment
07One path, two loadsSeries current, shared voltage, and whole-circuit changes.

Experience

Start with one 12 Ω training lamp on a 6 V source. Add a second identical lamp in the same path without changing the source. Compare brightness and measure current at several positions.

Next, use 12 Ω and 24 Ω resistors in series so brightness does not distract from the voltage comparison. Predict which has the larger drop, then measure both.

Learning target

Series means one path: the same current passes through both components. Adding series resistance reduces the current throughout the circuit. Voltage drops add to the source voltage; they are equal only for equal resistances in this case.

Notebook: add the series relation and an energy balance around the loop. Introduce Kirchhoff’s loop rule as conservation of energy, before expecting signed loop equations.

Solution & evidence

Two 12 Ω lamps across 6 V give Rtotal = 24 Ω, I = 0.25 A, and 3 V across each. Both glow dimly; this is a valid circuit. With 12 Ω + 24 Ω across 6 V, I ≈ 0.167 A and the drops are 2 V and 4 V. Reversing their order leaves those values unchanged.

Independent check: rearrange the unequal resistors. Use measurements to reject the claim that the first component gets more current or that each always gets half the voltage.

Tandem: “Both lamps became dimmer. Even the first one. The circuit has inconsiderately behaved as a whole system instead of waiting for electricity to encounter your changes in order.”

08Protect the instrumentApply Ohm’s law and a voltage balance to a design constraint.

Experience

Now revisit the existing Balance puzzle, with the missing prerequisites supplied. A 12 Ω training lamp is specified to operate at 6 V, but the station offers 12 V. Add one resistor to meet that specification. Meters remain available; the lamp’s target is clearly a device requirement.

After the first success, change the supply to 9 V. The player must adapt the design, not repeat a remembered resistor choice.

Learning target

Find the desired current from the load, then account for the remaining source voltage. The resistor changes the current in the entire series path. It does not remove charge.

Notebook: save a short design worked example: required load voltage → current → remaining voltage → added resistance. No new part appears here; this is consolidation.

Solution & evidence

The lamp needs I = 6/12 = 0.50 A. At a 12 V source the extra resistor must drop 6 V, so R = 6/0.50 = 12 Ω. At 9 V it must drop 3 V, so R = 6 Ω. Measure the lamp at 6 V in both arrangements and confirm the loop voltage balance.

Independent check: the 9 V version has a different physical arrangement and no default answer. Record the desired current and one confirming measurement. Hints can reveal the intermediate steps without penalizing the player.

Tandem: “I changed the supply voltage. Your parents paid for transferable knowledge, and I am contractually obliged to check that yours transfers.”

Room 05 · Shared light

Story: restore independent station services
09Independent branchesParallel voltage and current conservation at a junction.

Experience

A service lamp has its own switch. Add an emergency lamp that stays powered when that service branch is turned off. Both are 12 Ω training lamps on a 6 V source. First try the player’s design; then require the player to operate the switch.

Measure the two branch currents and the source current. Replace the service lamp with a 24 Ω test load to show that currents need not split equally.

Learning target

Parallel branches connect to the same pair of nodes and therefore have the same voltage. Current entering a junction equals current leaving it. An ideal voltage source supplies the total current demanded; its current is not a fixed amount divided among every possible circuit.

Notebook: add parallel connections and Kirchhoff’s junction rule. Node highlights appear only while inspecting connections.

Solution & evidence

Wire the emergency lamp directly across the source, with the switch only in the service branch. Both on: 6 V each, 0.50 A per branch, 1.00 A at the source. Service off: emergency stays at 6 V and 0.50 A; source current falls to 0.50 A. With a 24 Ω service load on, branch currents are 0.25 and 0.50 A; total 0.75 A.

Independent check: complete the unequal-branch measurement and actual switch test. The game must not substitute a hidden simulated switch operation for the player observing it.

Tandem: “You may now turn off one light without turning off the other. We use this advanced technology in almost every building your species has constructed.”

10The power budgetUse energy per second to choose an appropriate load.

Experience

The emergency branch stays on while the station adds a second service. Its training supply permits 4.5 W total. Compare 12 Ω and 24 Ω resistive modules on the 6 V bus; the modules have visible electrical power readouts and distinct equipment labels.

Show a timer-based energy tally as an optional observation, not a deadline. Running a device longer uses more energy even if its power stays the same.

Learning target

Power is energy transferred per second: P = IV. Current is charge per second. Voltage is energy per charge. The quantities connect but are not interchangeable.

Notebook: add watts and joules, P = IV and E = Pt for constant power. Use brightness comparisons only for comparable training lamps; never treat visual brightness as a universal current meter.

Solution & evidence

The existing 12 Ω lamp uses 6 × 0.50 = 3 W. A parallel 24 Ω module uses 6 × 0.25 = 1.5 W, giving 4.5 W total. A second 12 Ω module would give 6 W total and miss the equipment budget. The emergency lamp’s voltage remains 6 V in the ideal-source model.

Independent check: at fixed resistance, raising the source from 3 V to 6 V doubles current and quadruples power. Predict and measure on a separate, suitably rated test module. For the 24 Ω module at 6 V, ten seconds transfers 15 J.

Tandem: “The station has an energy budget. Your parents have a tuition budget. We are about to discover which one you respect first.”

Room 06 · Independence

Story: Tandem needs a competent partner to open the concourse
11Find the faultChoose evidence to distinguish an open path from a short.

Experience

Two training panels have gone dark. One has an open switch hidden among ordinary connectors; the other has an accidental wire bypass around its load and a tripped resettable fuse. Give a circuit diagram, access to meters, and visible connector geometry. No glowing “bad wire” marker.

The player proposes which observation would distinguish an opening from a bypass, inspects or measures, then repairs. All faults stay within the game’s protected training equipment.

Learning target

An open path blocks steady current. A short is a very low-resistance path; a bypass around a load can create excessive source current and trip protection. “Dark” alone does not diagnose either.

Notebook: save a compact fault comparison and the player’s confirming observation. Introduce the fuse as protection, not as unexplained punishment. Do not display post-trip zero current as proof that the original fault was an open circuit.

Solution & evidence

For the open-loop panel, verify the intact source voltage and a voltage difference across the opening, then reconnect it. For the shorted panel, use the trip indication and trace the wire that joins the source rails around the load; remove the bypass and reset the fuse. Confirm normal lamp voltage and current after each repair.

Independent check: present one rearranged fault panel. Require a relevant observation followed by a successful repair; record a short claim linked to the evidence. Avoid requiring a single predetermined measurement when several would work.

Tandem: “Two dark lamps. Two different faults. If your diagnosis is ‘the light is off,’ I will record it under observations and leave the diagnosis field empty.”

12Commission the stationCombine known ideas in a new, independent repair.

Experience

A new physical panel controls access to the larger concourse. An always-on beacon must stay at 6 V while a separate service lamp can be switched off. The panel supplies 12 V. Each lamp is a 12 Ω training load rated at 6 V; the source budget is 1.2 A.

The player receives these requirements and a schematic workspace, known tools, and a resistor tray. Tandem supplies context and responds when asked; he does not dictate the layout.

Learning target

Integrate circuit continuity, schematics, measurement, Ohm’s law, series voltage drops, parallel independence, and power. Introduce no new core mechanic.

Notebook: save the as-built circuit and measurements as the chapter’s final entry. Completion means foundation tasks demonstrated. The full AP exam and Earth-return authorization remain future story milestones.

One valid solution & evidence

Make two parallel branches across 12 V. Each contains a 12 Ω resistor in series with a 12 Ω lamp; add the switch only to the service branch. Each carries 0.50 A, each lamp receives 6 V, and total source current is 1.00 A with both on. With service off, the beacon remains at 6 V and total current falls to 0.50 A. Each resistor dissipates 3 W; specify training resistors rated comfortably above that, such as 5 W.

A single shared resistor before both branches does not meet the isolation requirement: the beacon voltage changes when the service branch opens. Accept every topology that satisfies the operating tests, not only a stored wire pattern.

Final check: the player actually tests both switch states, measures one lamp voltage and source current, and links an observation to a brief explanation. Offer an ungraded follow-up: with a 9 V source, each branch needs a 6 Ω series resistor to preserve 0.50 A and 6 V at the lamp. Use a fresh variant for an independent retry after detailed hints.

Tandem: “The concourse is yours. Your return authorization still requires the rest of the course, which your parents described as ‘a lovely opportunity.’ For what it is worth, I have updated my assessment of you. Do not make me read the previous version aloud.”

The reward stays spacious. Open the existing larger station for exploration after level 12. Let its restored lights, signage, observation windows, and quiet ambience make the repair feel consequential. Future lab doors say “Coming soon.” Do not place new compulsory lessons in the hub yet.

03 / Discovery and the notebook

Every entry should have a memory.

Discovering a battery should mean “I found out what this does.” The notebook should preserve the player’s experiment, then connect it to the vocabulary and representations they will need later.

1. Encounter

Inspect or first handle a new object. A short Tandem response names its purpose. Show one quiet “Battery discovered” notice; never stop play with a full-screen encyclopedia.

2. Use

The first meaningful action adds a concrete observation: “Completing the return path lit the lamp.” Using the object without inspecting it also records the entry, so no player misses essential notes.

3. Revisit

Later measurements add the symbol, units, equation, and one exam connection. Reuse the same page. Make advanced details available when learned.

Notebook example · after level 05

Battery

Job · Supplies energy to the circuit and maintains a potential difference between its terminals.

You observed · The meter read 6 V even when the switch was open and current was zero.

Exam connection · A voltage reading alone cannot tell you whether current flows.

At first discovery, show only the short job description and physical model. Add this symbol and the measured observation as those ideas are introduced.

Keep the existing four tabs

Parts retains the physical objects, with a short “What is a circuit?” introduction. Formulas gets concept-first notes and worked observations. Map stays spatial. Progression shows the twelve tasks grouped under six rooms.

Keep progress honest

Show “Complete” for demonstrated tasks. Store whether a transfer check was assisted, but do not shame hint use or display a dubious mastery percentage. Future lab counts are planning placeholders, locked and labeled “Coming soon,” not estimates of exam readiness.

Discovery is not a reading gate. The player should never have to open every notebook page to move on. Essential teaching happens through objects, feedback, measurements, and conversation; the notebook makes it easy to remember.

04 / Narrative and teaching

Tandem reacts to the experiment.

Keep the parents’ residential AP program premise. Tandem begins as an impeccably polite institutional nuisance, becomes visibly invested in the player’s progress, and finally admits that the player passing could help him visit Earth. His condescension stays funny because he is also observant, competent, and useful.

A story across all six rooms

  1. Wake: absurd intake bureaucracy; parents paid for the program.
  2. Contact: grudging permission to touch more equipment.
  3. Assembly: Tandem starts asking what the player expects, then listens to the result.
  4. Balance: a real instrument needs careful work; his concern briefly shows.
  5. Shared light: the repairs matter beyond the bench; his Earth ambitions slip out.
  6. Independence: he trusts the player with the concourse panel and frames future study as a shared project.

Conversation rules

E: a short exchange combining small talk with the current puzzle state. It can notice a particular connection or measurement without immediately revealing the solution.

T: a clear hint ladder: focus attention → suggest a useful experiment → explain the concept → give the exact next step on request. Keep this deterministic and authored.

Deliver multi-beat scenes around player actions. Pause while they manipulate a part. Avoid a long lecture, repeated jokes, or hints that refer to a circuit the player has already changed.

Example exchange · Level 04 · before measuring

“Your parents selected our comprehensive package. It includes meals, supervision, and an institutionally troubling number of lamps.”

“This one turns electrical energy into light and heat. The interesting question is whether it uses up the current in the process.”

“Put the meter in the path before the lamp. Then move it after the lamp. I could tell you what happens, but apparently the tuition was for you to learn.”

After the player obtains equal readings

“Half an ampere on both sides. The same charge passes each point per second. The lamp transfers energy; it does not consume the charge.”

“Keep that distinction. It is useful on exams, and it will spare us both a very tedious conversation later.”

E again · while the player is still exploring

“No, your parents do not get a notification every time you touch a wire. That was an early proposal. The server requirements were offensive.”

“If you want another useful test, open the switch and move the meter again. Ask whether the zero belongs to one location or to the entire loop.”

Humor should not punish curiosity. Let Tandem mock the institution, the situation, or his own extravagant confidence. After a mistake, explain what the player can observe next. Never make requesting help cost progress. Optional captions and notebook recall should preserve the lesson if dialogue is skipped.

These are tone and behavior samples for the proposal. The existing voice script and audio have not been rewritten or regenerated.

05 / AP alignment and boundaries

A foundation is not the whole unit.

AP expects students to work with representations, mathematics, experimental procedures, and explanations supported by evidence. The proposed chapter practices all four, but short physical tasks do not by themselves establish full written-exam readiness. College Board: science practices.

Proposed alignment to AP circuit content
Proposed levelsAP connectionLimit of this foundation
01–05Simple circuits, electric current, potential difference, representations and meter useIntroduces charge and energy qualitatively. Does not replace the preceding electric fields and potential material.
06–08Resistance and Ohm’s law; series networks; energy conservation around a loopResistivity, material properties, and a general signed-equation method still need development.
09–10Parallel networks, junction conservation, electrical powerIdeal sources and simple loads; no comprehensive treatment of real instruments or batteries.
11–12Compound DC circuits, experimental reasoning, explanation and transferA small range of faults and networks; more varied written problems and experiments are needed.

Topic comparison: AP Physics 2 CED, Unit 11 and AP Physics C: E&M CED, Unit 11. The lesson names and groupings here are our design choices.

Reserve a later “Circuits: Systems” chapter

Both course frameworks go beyond this foundation, including resistivity and RC circuits. Physics C also requires calculus-based treatments. Proposed later missions:

  1. Materials lab: investigate wire length, cross-sectional area, and resistivity; determine which variable changed.
  2. Real instruments: investigate source internal resistance, meter loading, and when the ideal assumptions fail.
  3. Networks: choose current directions, write signed loop and junction equations, and solve less familiar circuits.
  4. Stored energy: first establish capacitance, charge separation, and energy; then use capacitors in switching circuits.
  5. Change over time: measure charging and discharging curves and reason about initial and long-time behavior. Add differential equations and quantitative RC analysis in the appropriate Physics C route.

These are future scope, not new playable labs in this redesign. Before claiming complete AP coverage, map every required objective and course boundary to instruction and an assessment, including the additional mathematical depth of Physics C.

Why this teaching approach

The University of Washington’s circuit tutorials begin without assuming circuit knowledge: students investigate the contacts needed to light a bulb, construct a current model, and test predictions. That supports giving our objects and observations more attention before formal calculation. It does not prescribe this game’s exact twelve-level sequence. UW Physics Education Group: current and resistance tutorial.

The design recommendation is to make the puzzle itself carry the learning: constrained early tools, visible consequences, then less guidance and fresh arrangements. We should validate that recommendation with learners rather than assume that more dialogue, more levels, or a notebook automatically improves understanding.

06 / Implementation proposal

Build the teaching tools first.

  1. 1. Prototype levels 01–03.Implement meaningful discovery events, two-stage room flow, correct contact explanations, and a physical-to-schematic view. Observe beginners using these before rebuilding the whole chapter.
  2. 2. Add the missing instruments.Implement inline current measurement, two-probe voltage measurement, a selectable training supply, a small data table and graph, and explicit device ratings. Introduce each tool gradually.
  3. 3. Author and validate levels 04–10.Support new resistor values, unequal loads, actual switch experiments, measurement evidence, and changed-value transfer tasks. Keep one clear on-screen goal.
  4. 4. Finish faults, the capstone, and story timing.Validate several correct topologies and likely mistakes. Rewrite Tandem’s final scenes around stable interactions, then update the editable script and generate voice assets.
Implementation details to preserve scientific meaningImportant model and progression changes for the eventual build.
  • Separate powered from specified. The current success test treats a bulb as “lit” only near its rated voltage. Preserve visible dim illumination and current below that rating; say “below target” when a task requires 6 V. A dim lamp can still be in a valid circuit.
  • Explain the lamp model once. These are constant-resistance training lamps. Actual filament lamps are not generally ohmic as they heat. Do not use this simplified model as evidence that every real bulb obeys a fixed R.
  • Keep observations available. Stop automatically leaving the bench immediately after a first valid result. Let the player compare, measure, and choose when to step away.
  • Save demonstrations separately from visits. Store discovery events, observed comparisons, and completed transfer tasks. Retain completed learning records when a player experiments and breaks a previously working circuit.
  • Validate behavior, not a diagram template. Accept equivalent physical arrangements and all topologies meeting the operating requirements. Check both actual switch states where the task calls for isolation.
  • Be clear about electrical approximations. Sources and meters are ideal in the foundation. Use appropriate tolerances because the solver regularizes wires with small resistances. A virtual fuse and simplified lamp graphics should not silently change the model being taught.
  • Keep direction and energy distinct. Conventional-current arrows are a model, not electrons moving at the animation speed. Do not imply charge is used up or that a visible particle must travel from the battery before the lamp responds.
  • Protect existing progress. Version the redesigned campaign and offer a clear fresh start when it ships. Do not map old six-room completion directly to twelve new learning demonstrations. Dev god mode bypasses gates without marking learning as demonstrated.

How we will know it teaches better

Observe learning

Start with a small formative playtest of beginners and experienced students. Record where they hesitate, their explanations, the hints they request, and whether they can use a changed arrangement. Ask for short think-aloud explanations at selected checkpoints, not constant narration during play.

Give original, unseen problems before and after play and again after a delay. Include both physical and schematic representations. Score conceptual explanation separately from arithmetic and successful wiring.

Probe the known weak spots

  • Can they make a loop without copying?
  • Do they think a lamp consumes current?
  • Can voltage exist with no current?
  • Does adding a parallel branch dim the first lamp with an ideal source?
  • Can they choose meter connections and defend a diagnosis?
  • Can they explain why the capstone remains stable when one branch opens?

Release criterion: beginners should be able to explain and apply the target idea on a fresh task after support fades. If they succeed only by trying every wire or resistor, revise the teaching step. A small pilot diagnoses problems; it does not justify a claim that the game raises AP scores.

Recommended next step: approve the sequence conceptually, then prototype the first three levels and the discovery notebook. This document proposes the redesign; it does not change the running game.
