A guide to every lab.
From one wire to two paths.

Asterion’s six labs are small circuit puzzles. Each one introduces a new idea, then uses it to bring another part of the station back to life. Here’s what you’ll find, what to connect, and why it works.

How progression works

Walk to a bench and press E. Circuits update as you edit. A successful circuit lights the room and unlocks the next door automatically. The first restoration returns you to the room after a short pause; you can revisit the bench to experiment.

How to use this guide

Each chapter pairs exact connection steps with a solution diagram and a screenshot of the starting bench. Diagrams rearrange the parts for readability. In-game contact names are more reliable than “top,” “left,” or “right.”

01

Complete the path

Wake

Electric current needs a closed conducting loop.

The starting puzzle

A 6 V battery and one 6 V bulb are already placed. One wire joins battery positive to bulb contact A. The other two contacts are open.

Light the bulb by finishing the circuit.

The solution

  1. Select Wire, then click Battery negative.
  2. Click Bulb contact B to add the missing return wire. Dragging between those contacts works too.

Why it works

Both bulb contacts now lead to opposite battery terminals. The complete loop carries about 0.50 A, and the bulb receives about 6 V. A single lead cannot maintain current through the bulb.

Common wrong turn

Joining battery positive straight to battery negative makes a short circuit. Connect through the bulb instead.

RestoredThe bulb lights, Wake regains power, and the door to Contact unlocks.

02

Make contact

Contact

A switch completes or interrupts an existing path.

The starting puzzle

A 6 V battery, a bulb and a manual isolator are fully wired in one loop. The switch starts open.

Restore the existing circuit without adding parts.

The solution

  1. Click the switch body once to close it. Its accessible button is labeled Close switch.
  2. The existing wires already form the correct route. No extra wiring is needed.

Why it works

Closing the switch bridges its contacts. The bulb then receives about 6 V and the loop carries about 0.50 A. Opening it again interrupts the only path and turns the bulb off.

Common wrong turn

A wire around the switch can light the bulb, but bypassing an open switch does not solve this room. The switch must be closed and carry current.

RestoredThe bulb lights through the closed switch, and Assembly becomes accessible.

03

Build your first circuit

Assembly

The shape of the wiring can change; the connections are what matter.

The starting puzzle

The bench is empty. The tray offers one 6 V battery, one bulb and wires.

Construct a working circuit from loose parts.

The solution

  1. Choose Battery in the tray, then click an empty place on the board. Add a Bulb the same way.
  2. Connect Battery positive → Bulb contact A.
  3. Connect Battery negative → Bulb contact B.

Why it works

This recreates Wake’s complete loop using your own layout. The lamp is a simple resistive load, so exchanging its A and B connections also works. It still needs one connection to each battery terminal.

Common wrong turn

Placing components next to each other does not connect them. A crossing of two wires also does not create a junction; use the actual contacts.

RestoredThe bulb lights at about 6 V and 0.50 A, unlocking Balance.

04

Give the lamp just enough

Balance

Series resistance controls how the supply voltage is shared.

The starting puzzle

A fixed 12 V battery and a 6 V bulb have one wire already connected. You can add one resistor and choose 6 Ω, 12 Ω or 24 Ω.

Run the 6 V bulb correctly from a 12 V supply.

The solution

  1. Add a Resistor from the tray.
  2. Connect Battery negative → Resistor contact A, then Resistor contact B → Bulb contact B. Keep the existing positive-to-A wire.
  3. Select the resistor body and choose 12 Ω. The resistor initially defaults to 6 Ω.

Why it works

The game models the bulb as 12 Ω. At 6 V it needs I = V/R = 6/12 = 0.50 A. The resistor must drop the other 6 V at that same current: R = V/I = 6/0.50 = 12 Ω. Total resistance is 24 Ω, so the 12 V supply splits about equally.

What the three resistor values do
ResistorLamp voltageResult
6 Ω≈ 8 VOverloaded
12 Ω≈ 6 VCorrect
24 Ω≈ 4 VToo dim

Common wrong turn

A resistor wired in parallel with the bulb does not reduce the voltage across it. Put the resistor in the same unbranched path, and remove any wire that bypasses it.

RestoredThe lamp operates near 6 V and the resistor carries current. The door to Shared light unlocks.

05

Share twelve volts

Shared light

Two equal loads in series divide the supply voltage equally.

The starting puzzle

Only a fixed 12 V battery is on the bench. The tray lets you add two 6 V bulbs, each modeled as 12 Ω.

Light both bulbs correctly from the same battery.

The solution

  1. Add two bulbs. You may rotate them to make the wires easier to follow.
  2. Connect Battery negative → Bulb 1 contact A.
  3. Connect Bulb 1 contact B → Bulb 2 contact B, then Bulb 2 contact A → Battery positive.

Why it works

There is one route through both bulbs: their resistances add to 24 Ω. The current is 12/24 = 0.50 A everywhere in that route. Each 12 Ω bulb drops about 6 V. Current is not used up by the first bulb.

Common wrong turn

Putting both bulbs directly across the 12 V battery makes a parallel circuit. Each would receive 12 V, twice its rating, so both are overloaded. This room calls for series wiring.

RestoredBoth bulbs operate near 6 V together, unlocking Independence.

06

Give each light its own path

Independence

Parallel branches share voltage and can be interrupted independently.

The starting puzzle

A 6 V battery already powers Lamp 1 through a closed isolator switch. You can add a second bulb.

Light both bulbs, while ensuring Lamp 2 stays lit when Lamp 1’s switch opens.

The solution

  1. Add the second Bulb and keep the existing branch wiring.
  2. Connect Battery positive → Bulb 2 contact A and Battery negative → Bulb 2 contact B. Several wires may share a battery contact.
  3. Leave the original switch closed for completion. After restoration, reopen the bench and click Open switch to see Lamp 1 go dark while Lamp 2 stays lit.

Why it works

Both branches connect across the same 6 V source, so each bulb gets about 6 V. With both on, each branch carries about 0.50 A and the battery supplies about 1.00 A in total. Opening the first branch leaves about 0.50 A flowing through the second.

Common wrong turn

Wiring Lamp 2 in series with Lamp 1 gives each only about 3 V. Putting the switch in a shared return switches both off. Bypassing the isolator also fails: Lamp 1 must actually go dark when it opens.

RestoredThe game checks both bulbs, then internally opens the isolator to verify independence. Passing this check unlocks the observation deck.

The science, in three lines

The formulas behind the puzzles

Ohm’s law

V = I × R

Voltage V is measured in volts, current I in amperes, and resistance R in ohms. A 12 Ω lamp at 6 V carries 0.50 A.

Series

Vₛ = V₁ + V₂

One path means the same current through every part. Resistances add. Equal resistances share the supply voltage equally.

Parallel

V₁ = V₂ = Vₛ

Each branch spans the same two supply nodes. Branch currents add at the battery; opening one branch can leave the other intact.

Back at the bench

When a circuit fails

The bulb is dark

Trace a continuous route from one battery terminal, through the bulb, and back to the other terminal. Check for a missing lead, an open switch, or a bypass around the lamp.

The bulb glows but the door stays shut

Visible light alone is not enough. Every required bulb must be near its rated voltage. Balance also needs current through a resistor; Contact needs it through the closed switch; Independence needs separate paths.

“Short circuit” appears

A low-resistance path across the source trips the virtual protection. Undo the last wire or remove the bypass. The circuit retries automatically after an edit.

A part or wire is in the wrong place

Drag a movable part by its body. Select it and press R to rotate. Select an added part or wire and use Delete to remove it. The fixed starter parts stay in place. Undo and Reset circuit are available.

You need the controls or a reminder

Move with WASD, run with Shift, and look with the mouse or arrow keys. Open the notebook with N or its map with M. Esc cancels a pending lead, leaves the bench, or opens pause, depending on context.

A solved circuit was changed

The door stays unlocked after the room’s first valid restoration, even if you undo, reset, or change its circuit. You can return and experiment without losing access to the next room.

About the numbers. These puzzles model every bulb as a fixed 12 Ω resistor rated for 6 V. Real filament lamps change resistance as they heat; this game uses a simpler teaching model. The displayed calculations round tiny losses in the 0.001 Ω wires and closed switches. A bulb counts as correctly lit between 94% and 106% of its rated voltage (5.64–6.36 V for these bulbs), and a battery current above 4 A trips the virtual fuse.

The two interactive diagrams use the same rounded teaching values. They illustrate resistor choice and branch isolation; they do not change your game save.

After chamber 06

The observation deck is yours.

The final repair opens the observation deck, where ASTER welcomes you back. There is no seventh circuit puzzle. You have gone from completing a single loop to building a supply with independent branches.

Scope: This guide covers the current six-chamber Asterion game. Earlier labs such as Materials workshop, Life support, Reserve vault and Airlock control remain in older source modules but are not part of this playable sequence.
